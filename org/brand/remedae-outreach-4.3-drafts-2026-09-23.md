### 1. Prof Michael Heinrich, UCL

**Subject:** A question about how we grade herbal evidence

Dear Professor Heinrich,

Remedae is a UK health library that puts every healing tradition beside modern medicine for one condition, with each remedy graded on evidence and quoted to its source. We have ingested the Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India and the West African Herbal Pharmacopoeia and cite them page by page.

The part I am least sure we have right is the grading. Our five evidence words run from "documented lineage" to "clinical guideline", and herbal remedies sit awkwardly across them: strong pharmacognosy, thin trials. Your work on exactly that gap, and the ethics committee lens you bring to it, is why I am writing.

Would you give us twenty-five minutes to pull the labels apart? If it turns out useful, a critique from your group, or a student project on the question, would be welcome, but the conversation is the ask.

---

### 2. Prof Elisabeth Hsu, Oxford

**Subject:** What survives when Chinese medicine is written in plain English

Dear Professor Hsu,

Remedae is a health library that sets Chinese medicine, Kampo, Ayurveda and modern medicine side by side for a single condition, in plain English, with every remedy quoted to its source. We rewrite pattern language ("liver qi stagnation", "kidney yang deficiency") for a general reader, and I worry about what that translation costs.

Your fieldwork on Chinese medicine in East Africa is about that exact movement of knowledge across languages and systems, which is why I am writing rather than to a clinician.

Would you spare twenty-five minutes to look at two of our TCM pages and tell us where the translation goes wrong? If you know one or two early-career researchers who would enjoy taking that further, an introduction would mean a lot.

---

### 3. Prof Claudia Witt, Zurich

**Subject:** How people read integrative evidence on a screen

Dear Professor Witt,

I run Remedae, a digital health library that presents modern medicine and established traditional systems for the same condition, each remedy graded on evidence and linked to its source. We are a small UK team with a pilot cohort using it now.

Your combination of integrative medicine and digital health at Zurich is rare, and the question I most want a second opinion on sits exactly there: when a reader sees a guideline-grade card next to a "documented lineage" card, what do they actually take from it? We have made choices about grading and layout without anyone in your field checking them.

Would you give us twenty-five minutes for a methodology critique? We would share the pipeline, the audit we ran on our own sources, and the pilot's reading data.

---

### 4. Prof Helena Teede, Monash

**Subject:** PMOS, as a patient reads it

Dear Professor Teede,

Congratulations on the renaming. Remedae, a UK health library that shows how modern medicine and traditional systems approach the same condition, updated its PCOS pages to carry the PMOS name the week the Lancet paper appeared, with the old name kept for search.

The question I would value your view on is narrow: how should a consumer page explain the new name in the three-year transition, without confusing the women who arrive searching for PCOS? We have a draft explanation and a journal piece, and I would rather get the wording right from you than guess.

Fifteen minutes, or written comments on one page, would be plenty. If MCHRI ever wants a lay-facing test bed for the terminology, we have one running.

---

### 5. Prof Krina Zondervan, Oxford

**Subject:** What endometriosis patients most need explained accurately

Dear Professor Zondervan,

Remedae is a UK health library that gathers, for one condition, what modern medicine and the traditional systems say, graded on evidence and cited to source. Our endometriosis page sits on a women's health shelf that is one of the most-read parts of the site.

I am writing with one question rather than a proposal: from twenty-five years of the epidemiology, what do people with endometriosis most often have explained to them wrongly, and what would you want a plain-English page to say first? Your 2026 genetic study and the CaRe centre's work are the background to that question.

Twenty minutes, or a few lines by email, would shape the page directly. Anything larger, a methodology critique or a student project, only if you see a fit.

### 6. Prof Cassandra Quave, Emory

**Subject:** How a traditional remedy becomes a testable question

Dear Professor Quave,

Remedae is a UK health library that presents traditional remedies beside modern medicine for the same condition, with each one graded on evidence and quoted to its source. Roughly a third of our cards rest on documented use with no trial behind them, and we say so on the card. We keep that list on purpose.

Your work is the clearest account I know of how a remedy moves from field notes to a lab question, and that is the gap our register describes. I would like to ask you how you would prioritise it.

Would you give us twenty-five minutes? And separately, if Foodie Pharmacology ever wants a guest on evidence across traditions, I would be glad to be one.

### 8. Prof Sushrut Jadhav, UCL

**Subject:** Different models of illness on one page

Dear Professor Jadhav,

Remedae is a UK health library that shows, for one condition, how modern medicine, Ayurveda, Unani, Chinese medicine and others each understand and treat it, side by side and in plain English. The site holds them as equal readings rather than ranking them.
